# Recipes: complete landing pages section by section

Skeletons to start from — not templates to fill. Text in brackets is replaced with facts about the product; if a
bracket lacks evidence, remove the promotional claim or explain the unresolved limitation when it affects the
decision. Each recipe states when it fits, the reasoning
behind the order, and what to cut first.

Before using a recipe, do the [strategy](01-strategy-and-structure.md): goal, audience, stage, objections and proof.
A recipe chosen without them produces the generic page this skill tries to avoid.

## How to use a recipe

1. Pick the recipe closest to the page type and the visitor's stage.
2. Map each section to a visitor question and to proof you actually have.
3. Remove unsupported promotion; keep material limitations visible; merge duplicate answers.
4. Reorder for the visitor's stage (comparison earlier for switchers, pricing earlier for ready-to-buy visitors).
5. Write all the copy in a document before designing (see [persuasive writing](04-persuasive-writing.md)).

## Software for teams (SaaS)

**Fits:** self-serve or hybrid software, problem-aware visitors, a product that can be shown.

1. **Navigation**: logo · Product · Pricing · Customers · Resources · Sign in · [Try free]
2. **Hero**: "[Outcome] for [audience]" · "[Product] [does X] so that [benefit]." · [Try free] [Watch a 2-min demo]
   · "Free up to [limit]. No card." · a large real screenshot of the main workflow.
3. **Logos**: "Used by [N] teams in [industries]" + 6–8 real logos, with permission.
4. **Before / now**: four parallel rows of today's pain and how it changes.
5. **How it works**: three steps with a screenshot each.
6. **Features**: a grid with one large card (the key feature) + four medium ones, each with a real fragment.
7. **Integrations**: "Works with what you already use" + logos by category + a link to the directory.
8. **Proof**: two or three cases with a figure ("[Customer] reduced [metric] by [N] %"), a person with photo and role.
9. **Security**: certifications with their meaning + where data lives.
10. **Pricing summary**: three plans + "See the full comparison".
11. **FAQ**: six to eight real objections.
12. **Closing**: "[The hero's idea said differently]" + button + risk reducers.
13. **Footer**: site map, legal, status, language, social.

**Why this order:** visitors first confirm what it is (hero), that others trust it (logos), that it addresses their
pain (before/now), that it is not hard (how it works), and what it does (features); objections follow once interest
exists.

**Cut first:** before/now if the hero already names the pain; a separate security section when the remaining
disclosures answer the actual risks; integrations if there
are few.

**Variants:** sales-led → replace "Try free" with "Book a demo", move security and case studies up. Developer tools →
add a code sample or playground after the hero, documentation link in the navigation.

## AI tool (generation)

**Fits:** tools whose output can be shown; visitors skeptical about quality, control and data.

1. **Hero**: a headline about what is created ("Product videos ready for social in minutes") + a prompt field or a
   live example + a gallery of real results below.
2. **Gallery by use case**, with each result's prompt, copyable.
3. **Input → output**: one example step by step (what you upload, what you get, how long it takes).
4. **Control**: what can be adjusted (style, brand, length, later editing) with screenshots.
5. **For whom**: three audiences with the piece each one makes.
6. **Trust**: usage rights, privacy (no training on your data, if true), honest limits.
7. **Pricing** by credits or generations with a simple calculator.
8. **Social proof** from creators with their work.
9. **Closing**: "Try it with your first [piece]" + button.

**Why this order:** quality is the first doubt, so outputs come first; control and trust follow because they are the
next objections. See [AI, physical products and portfolios](12-ai-physical-products-and-portfolios.md).

## Physical product

**Fits:** a single hero product or a small range; direct-to-consumer.

1. **Hero**: photo or 3D of the object at its best angle + name + one line + price and [Buy].
2. **In use**: photo or video of the object in its real context, with three benefits.
3. **Detail**: materials, dimensions and weight annotated over the image.
4. **How it is used**: three steps or a short video.
5. **Comparison** with the alternative it replaces (no brand names unless necessary and fair).
6. **Reviews** with customer photos and a score with the number of reviews.
7. **Shipping, returns, warranty** in three columns.
8. **FAQ** + **closing** with the buy button and the price.

**Why this order:** desire first (object, use), then detail for rational confirmation, then risk reduction close to the
purchase.

## Waitlist

**Fits:** pre-launch products; the goal is qualified sign-ups and learning.

1. **Hero**: what it is (one sentence), when (month or season), [email] [Reserve my spot], "[N] people signed up" (if
   real).
2. **What you will be able to do**: three concrete things with an image or preview.
3. **Why sign up now**: early access, founder pricing (if real).
4. **Who is making it**: the team or the story in a paragraph.
5. **Closing** with the same field + a minimal footer.

See [mobile, launches and integrations](10-mobile-launches-and-integrations.md) for states after sign-up.

## Studio or portfolio

**Fits:** agencies, studios, freelancers; visitors judging quality and fit.

1. **Hero**: a positioning sentence ("We design digital products for [kind of clients]") + the best piece in motion.
2. **Selected work**: six to eight projects with image, client, discipline and result.
3. **Services** in a short list with what each includes.
4. **Process**: three or four stages.
5. **Clients and recognition**.
6. **Contact**: copyable email + "Book a call" + the kinds of projects they take.

## Comparison page ("[Product] vs. [Competitor]" or "[Competitor] alternative")

**Fits:** visitors actively comparing; search traffic for competitor names.

1. **Hero**: "The [competitor] alternative for [audience]" + the main difference in one line + [Try free].
2. **Summary table**: five to eight verifiable differences, with the date of the comparison.
3. **Why teams switch**: three reasons with customer quotes from people who switched.
4. **Where [competitor] may be better**: honest; it builds trust.
5. **Migration**: how to move, what is imported, how long it takes.
6. **Pricing comparison** for equivalent plans.
7. **Closing**.

**Rules:** facts only, sourced and dated; no mockery; update when the competitor changes; follow local law on
comparative advertising.

## Mobile app

**Fits:** consumer apps; most traffic on phones.

1. **Hero**: what the app does + the app in a phone in real use + store buttons (by platform) or QR on desktop.
2. **Three moments of use** with screenshots.
3. **Ratings and reviews** from the stores (real, current).
4. **Privacy**: what data is collected (consistent with store labels).
5. **Closing** with store buttons.

## Event or webinar

**Fits:** a dated event with registration.

1. **Hero**: title, date and time with time zone (and "add to calendar"), format (online, place), [Register].
2. **What you will learn**: three concrete outcomes.
3. **Speakers** with photos, roles and one line each.
4. **Agenda** with times.
5. **Registration form** (few fields) + what happens after (link, reminder, recording).
6. **After the event**: offer the recording if permitted and available; otherwise explain what remains accessible.

## Before writing code

- Write all the copy in a document and read it straight through: is it understood without images?
- Choose the visual idea (see [art direction and creativity](05-art-direction-and-creativity.md)).
- Mark the two to four motion moments (see [motion](06-motion.md)).
- Collect real proof (screenshots, figures, permissions for logos and testimonials).
- Decide the performance budget and the reduced-motion behavior.
- Test the skeleton with the longest language and on a phone.

## Decide which recipe to challenge

A recipe is a hypothesis about the order of questions. The useful starting point is not “this is SaaS,” but
“this visitor needs to judge migration risk before starting a trial.” Category predicts some concerns; arrival
intent, commitment and available evidence determine their priority.

### Questions before assembling sections

- Which promise brought the visitor here, and where is that exact concern answered?
- Is the person discovering, comparing, seeking approval or returning to take action?
- Who else will use this page as evidence: a colleague, purchaser, guardian or security reviewer?
- Which facts could legitimately make the visitor decide not to proceed?
- Is there enough proof to support a long argument, or only one demonstrable capability?
- Does the primary action remain available in every market served by the page?
- Who will maintain dates, prices, screenshots and eligibility after the campaign ends?

If there are several decision makers, make their evidence reachable through meaningful navigation. That does not
necessarily require a different hero for each role. A shared proposition with clearly labeled detail can preserve
coherence; separate pages help when the tasks or commitments genuinely differ.

| Situation | Structure | Reason | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Returning buyer | Action and terms first | Removes repeated education | Less brand storytelling |
| New unfamiliar category | Explanation before proof | Makes evidence interpretable | Longer route to action |
| High switching risk | Migration early | Answers a blocking concern | More technical detail |
| Several distinct services | Choice of task first | Avoids a false universal promise | Adds a choice |
| Narrow announcement | Short factual page | Matches limited evidence | Less room for broader discovery |
| Product cannot serve visitor | Honest eligibility route | Prevents wasted effort | Fewer nominal conversions |

Do not use a long software recipe for a known customer's renewal notice. Do not use a minimalist waitlist recipe
for a paid preorder without delivery, cancellation and payment terms. Similar visual components can support very
different commitments; the content obligations follow the commitment.

## Build a section dependency map

Write each section's question, answer, evidence and next unresolved doubt. Read only those four fields in sequence.
If the next section presumes something not yet explained, reorder or add the missing explanation. If two adjacent
sections reach the same conclusion from the same evidence, merge them.

The numbers of cards, steps, logos and projects in the recipes are illustrative composition choices. They are not
minimum content requirements. Two relevant cases can be stronger than eight weak ones; a complex setup may need
more than three honest steps. Do not compress operational work to make a diagram symmetrical.

A section can have a useful role without persuading. Eligibility, exclusions and compatibility help people reject
an unsuitable product. Evaluate those sections by decision accuracy, not by whether they increase clicks.
When evidence is unavailable, describe the supported capability and limitation; do not substitute a testimonial.

### Editing order under constraints

Start with factual validity and the promised action. Next repair missing decision information, then order and
navigation. Tune visual rhythm after the argument holds. This sequence avoids polishing a beautiful section that
later disappears because its claim cannot be supported.

When a deadline forces cuts, remove decorative repetition before recovery instructions or material terms.
A static screenshot may replace a live demo if it proves the same point; it cannot prove performance or reliability
that was never measured. Name what the replacement demonstrates and what it leaves unresolved.

## States of the whole page

Page recipes often describe a single launch-day snapshot. Campaigns change, services fill up and third-party
systems fail. Define those transitions before sharing the page widely, because old links continue circulating.

| State | Recipe adaptation | What the visitor must retain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-launch | Preview and explicit availability | Difference between interest and purchase |
| Limited availability | Eligibility or inventory near action | Honest route for unsupported cases |
| Demo unavailable | Static explanation and retry option | Enough information to decide |
| Evidence withdrawn | Remove or replace the affected claim | A coherent argument without false proof |
| Offer expired | Current terms or dated archive | Clear distinction from the old offer |
| Event postponed | Revised date and attendee instructions | What happens to an existing registration |
| Service retired | Explanation and relevant alternatives | A way to resolve existing commitments |
| Partial localization | Mark unavailable content or routes | Language choice without a misleading promise |

Keep a map from each campaign action to its actual destination. A closing button and a hero button can drift to
different plans after a pricing change. Check repeated actions as one contract, including tracking parameters,
region selection and the state reached after authentication.

## Accessibility and localization of the sequence

A persuasive sequence should also make sense in document order. WCAG 1.3.2 Meaningful Sequence (A) applies when
order affects meaning; a desktop layout that visually reverses alternating rows must retain a comprehensible
reading sequence. Do not rely on arrows or spatial position alone to explain the steps.

WCAG 2.4.6 Headings and Labels (AA) requires descriptive headings and labels. Descriptive section headings are
especially important on long recipes because people may jump directly to cost, eligibility or compatibility.
A single page-level heading is a useful convention, not a substitute for reviewing the hierarchy.

WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow (AA) applies at the specified narrow viewport with exceptions for genuinely two-dimensional
content. Collapse decorative columns without making the person pan across the whole page. Comparison tables may
need contained scrolling and clear row labels rather than hiding the less flattering columns.

Market adaptation can reorder the argument. A market without a supported payment method needs eligibility before
purchase enthusiasm; an event serving several regions needs time-zone clarity near every registration decision.
Language and region selectors solve different problems and should not silently change one another.

Avoid writing headlines as fragments distributed across cards. Complete messages let translators change word
order and agreement. Test scripts without spaces, long headings and RTL reading order before approving a section
count. A translated sentence taking another line is not a reason to remove necessary meaning.

## Worked comparison: choosing a recipe rather than filling it

Fictional example: a team scheduling tool serves clinic administrators replacing a spreadsheet. Its supported
capabilities are importing appointment lists, assigning practitioners and sending configured reminders. There is
no measured claim about reducing missed appointments, and no authorized customer testimonial yet.

**Weak:** follow the software recipe literally: six anonymous logos, three outcome figures, a “trusted by clinics”
headline, an AI gallery because it looks current, then a trial form. This gives the page a familiar rhythm while
making its least supportable claims most prominent. It also leaves import compatibility unanswered.

**Strong:** use a factual hero, an import preview with supported columns, an example scheduling workflow, clear
reminder limits, plan terms and the trial action. Explain which data cannot be imported. Leave out the logo strip
and results case until they exist. Put privacy detail close to the invitation to upload a sample.

The stronger structure costs some visual variety and social reassurance. It earns credibility by showing the
actual evaluation task. If later research finds administrators already understand migration and mostly compare
prices, move equivalent-plan pricing earlier; do not defend the original order because it is now polished.

A second reasonable option is a sales-assisted evaluation for complex imports. Reject it for straightforward
self-serve files if it creates unnecessary delay. Choose it when unsupported source systems require human review,
and label the action as requesting help rather than starting an instant import.

## Verification and measurement

Test the outline before the artwork. Ask a participant to find what would prevent them from using the product,
explain the next step and identify which claim they would need to verify. This reveals gaps that a visual
preference test cannot. Use the same task and facts when comparing two orders.

Then test the assembled page with media unavailable, narrow widths, keyboard navigation and long translations.
Follow the action through confirmation. Include a visitor who should not convert; the page should help them
recognize a mismatch without misleading or trapping them.

Define success at the destination: a useful trial task, an attended consultation or a correctly completed purchase.
Track disappointed expectations, returns and unsupported requests as guardrails. Scroll depth can explain exposure,
but reaching the footer is not inherently success and leaving after finding an answer is not inherently failure.

When traffic is limited, use observed misunderstandings to revise the structure and report the uncertainty.
A before/after change also changes time, traffic and campaign conditions; do not attribute all movement to the recipe.
Record what result would justify changing the order again, such as a repeated unresolved compatibility question.

## Checklist

- [ ] The chosen recipe follows visitor intent and commitment, not only product category.
- [ ] Every section has a question, an answer and an evidence status.
- [ ] Counts of cards, steps and proof items reflect actual content.
- [ ] Material limitations remain visible when promotional proof is missing.
- [ ] Expiry, failure and unsupported-market states have a coherent route.
- [ ] Reading order and localization preserve the argument.
- [ ] The downstream outcome and guardrails can challenge the chosen structure.

## Common mistakes

Recipes become rigid when a team mistakes a familiar sequence for evidence. Empty proof sections then create
pressure to invent numbers or add irrelevant logos. Keep the question-to-evidence map beside the outline and
require a reason for each retained section. Reuse layout components without inheriting unsupported claims.


- **Using a recipe without strategy.**
- **Keeping sections without proof** because the recipe has them.
- **Filling brackets with invented content.**
- **Never reordering for the visitor's stage.**
