# Audience pages, security and trust, and the About page

Three kinds of content that answer "is this for someone like me?", "can I trust you with my data?" and "who are you?".
Each is often handled as an afterthought — a grid of industries, a row of badges, a paragraph of history — and each
can carry real persuasive weight when designed from the visitor's question.

## Sections and pages by audience, role or industry

### When to segment

Segment when audiences differ in **problem, proof or objection**, not just in name. If the only change between
"For agencies" and "For startups" is the word, segmentation adds clicks without meaning.

| Signal | Segment? |
| --- | --- |
| Different jobs to be done (sales vs. support teams) | Yes, by role |
| Different regulations or vocabulary (banking, healthcare) | Yes, by industry |
| Different buying process (self-serve vs. procurement) | Yes, by company size |
| Same problem, same proof, different label | No — one page with varied examples |

### Patterns

- **Choose the audience and show its benefit**: tabs or cards by role that change the content, with that role's
  figures ("Sales reps: +60 % meetings · 10 h saved on research";
  [Amplemarket](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/85de217d-baba-4edb-8805-3d2a76f2244b)).
- **Few options with a stance** ("For teams that publish, measure and win": Enterprise · Agencies · Startups, the
  chosen one open with what matters to that audience;
  [Ploy](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/2c493008-c3a6-42fc-b5a9-ed736484cb85) ).
- **An industry-specific sentence**, not the same sentence with another word ("The calls that hurt most go
  unanswered" for banking; [Giga](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3d319855-f6a3-4f40-ab76-cf4cc860b765)).
- **An image of the audience's real context** with a product fragment over it
  ([Slash](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/be15de50-350a-4226-b2ce-0d02cbef1b4a)).
- **When there are many, a compact grid** with an icon and one line, separating "Solutions" (by team) from
  "Industries" ([Canva](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/e1809f9a-8dd4-4011-938b-4b3f1f1e863b)).
- **Give an audience a separate page when its argument needs depth**: specific hero, evidence and objections.
  A shared overview can retain concise answers so visitors do not have to choose a label before understanding fit.
- **Anti-example**: industry cards with abstract images and "Learn more" with no data from the industry
  ([Zendesk](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/a7beb694-c7f2-4d1e-bc8f-b109efb061c7)).

### What an audience page needs

- A hero written in that audience's vocabulary, naming their problem.
- Proof from that audience: their industry's customers, their size, their region.
- The features that matter to them first; the rest linked.
- Their specific objections (compliance for healthcare, client management for agencies).
- An action that fits their buying process (trial for small teams, demo for enterprise).

### Tabs vs. pages

Tabs on the main landing can provide a quick overview; separate pages support depth and shareable destinations.
If using tab semantics, implement their expected keyboard and selection behavior. Ordinary links or disclosure
sections may be simpler. Essential information needs a usable path if the enhancement fails.

## Security and trust

### What visitors need to know

Trust content answers concrete questions from buyers, IT and legal reviewers: where is data stored, who can access
it, is it encrypted, is it used to train AI, what happens if something goes wrong, which certifications apply, how to
report a vulnerability.

### Patterns

- **Certifications with what they mean**, not only the badge: "SOC 2 Type II: our controls were audited by a third
  party" ([Dovetail](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3ee471e1-fbad-429e-953e-57a309b5ebfd),
  [Miro](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/49ef29d7-9efd-4672-9784-cef76f57ebe1)).
- **Honest status**: "In progress", "Pending" when not yet obtained
  ([Serus](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/d674ddba-26f4-40ad-a39f-f710227c0085),
  [Customer.io](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/4d7ccf09-e699-46f8-b59c-a0b8ac745dec)).
- **Concrete handling facts**: encryption at rest and in transit, where data is stored (choose EU or US), who accesses
  it, whether it trains AI ([Sana](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/7a8c2178-cea1-4a2d-b91d-780654319e23)).
- **A link to the trust center** (policies, subprocessors, reports, service status, vulnerability disclosure).
- **Third-party product reviews** can answer service-quality concerns when sourced and current, but they do not
  prove security controls or replace an audit report.
- **Placement**: near the relevant commitment and in navigation or the footer. Bring security into the hero when
  it is a decisive differentiator or first objection, even if the product is not itself a security tool.

### Tone

Calm and precise. Security copy that sounds like marketing ("military-grade encryption", "bank-level security")
reduces trust with technical reviewers. Say what is done, in plain terms, with links to details.

### Markets

Applicable obligations depend on jurisdiction, sector, data and the service relationship. Have the responsible
legal and security owners verify market-specific claims. Residency, privacy controls, contracts and certifications
answer different questions; one is not a substitute for the others.

## The About page

### What visitors look for

Prospective customers check that a real, stable team exists; candidates look for culture and mission; journalists and
partners look for facts. The page should serve all three quickly.

### Patterns

- **The mission in one concrete sentence** as the headline ("We're building documentation for the age of intelligent
  systems"; [GitBook](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/b8ff9683-7390-4621-8ea3-22a7f0a92c8c)).
- **A short, specific origin story** (who, what problem they lived, when;
  [Amplemarket](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f7489dc4-e65f-4ba7-818c-543d503ed9df)).
- **A timeline** with milestones and a photo for each moment
  ([Mews](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/8b7d9d81-339d-4b24-a381-476d6d1b347f),
  [Handshake](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/ec153601-7b86-4380-946e-655696b65bbd)).
- **The real team** in real photos (not stock avatars), and facts that give scale ("5 years, 30+ people";
  [Fibery](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3ee821a0-7e64-4eb1-b05f-8f147cc3ef7c) does it with humor).
- **Values with examples** of how they are applied, not a list of words.
- **Next step**: open roles, press kit, contact, office addresses.
- **Anti-example**: a long block of text on a colored background with no images or structure
  ([Quicken](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3e479ea1-bede-431d-8b4a-7c244ce2b3f7)).

### Team photos and privacy

Ask each person for consent to appear; offer an option without a photo; keep the list current when people leave.

## Questions before choosing the story

Audience, trust and About content serve distinct doubts. A visitor can recognize their role yet still question
whether the vendor can support the required workflow. Another can trust the vendor's competence but discover
that the product is unavailable in their market. Separate these doubts before choosing the visual treatment.

- Who needs this information: the operator, economic buyer, security reviewer, candidate or journalist?
- Which audience differences change the product workflow or the buying process, rather than only the vocabulary?
- Can a person belong to several groups, and what happens if none of the labels feels accurate?
- What evidence exists for each audience claim, including markets and organization sizes not yet represented?
- Which trust facts are public, restricted, dated, pending or dependent on a particular plan?
- Who approves wording about controls, audit scope, locations and subprocessors, and who keeps it current?
- Is the About page proving continuity, craft, mission, public accountability or recruiting fit?
- Which team members or customers have agreed to appear, and what happens when that permission changes?

A logo, badge or biography can support recognition, but it cannot answer every doubt. Write the actual question
next to each proposed section; if the evidence does not answer it, change the evidence or narrow the claim.

## Alternatives and when not to segment

| Situation | Option | Why | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shared job across roles | One argument, varied examples | Less self-classification | Less targeted search |
| Different workflows and proof | Dedicated audience pages | Makes relevance concrete | More content to maintain |
| Several buyers, one workflow | Shared page, role detail | Coherent story | Careful hierarchy needed |
| Occasional diligence | Summary and trust center | Scanning and depth | Keep both consistent |
| Restricted reports | Explain scope and request path | Clear access boundary | Procurement friction |
| Young company | Team capability and honest status | Available evidence | Limited historical proof |
| Confidential identities | Role and capability summaries | Privacy and substance | Less personal recognition |

Do not multiply pages merely because every competitor has an industry menu. A thin page can promise expertise
the product does not have. Use one honest workflow page until industry differences and credible evidence justify
separate arguments. Search demand alone does not create product suitability.

Similarly, do not use an employee photo grid as the only proof of company stability. For some teams publication
is unsafe or unwanted. Clear ownership, contact routes, service commitments and relevant experience can do more.

## Build the argument from evidence

1. Map each visitor role to its decision: fit, approval, contact, employment or verification.
2. List the objections that genuinely change that decision, and identify their evidence owners.
3. Separate shared facts from audience-specific claims; shared facts should have a consistent source.
4. Choose the shallowest structure that lets each visitor resolve their doubt without guessing.
5. Put a concise answer near the commitment and link to depth where the visitor needs to verify it.
6. Establish update triggers: audit renewal, region launch, organizational change, control change or staff departure.
7. Test whether the visitor can explain both the claim and its limit before presenting the page as persuasive.

For a security statement, record the entity, service, scope, date and source behind the wording. "Encrypted" is
incomplete if the visitor needs to know whether it covers transport, storage, backups or a particular deployment.
Do not invent technical detail to fill the gap; ask the owner and publish only the supported scope.

Audit reports, certificates and self-described practices differ in what they establish. Use the actual document's
terminology and reviewed status rather than treating every badge as a certification. An "in progress" badge is
not an earned assurance and should not be visually indistinguishable from completed evidence.

## States and edge cases

| State | Risk | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No matching audience label | Visitor assumes exclusion | Offer a workflow route or general overview |
| Visitor has several roles | Contradictory recommendations | Allow movement between pages with shared facts intact |
| No customer evidence for a segment | Unsupported expertise claim | Show a relevant demonstration with its limits |
| Report requires access | Dead end or opaque sales capture | Explain who can request it and what follows |
| Report request pending | Approval mistaken for completion | Show receipt and genuine response expectation |
| Audit or policy being renewed | Old badge appears current | State verified status and applicable dates |
| Regional feature unavailable | Audience page overpromises | Surface availability before the conversion action |
| Incident underway | Reassurance conflicts with reality | Link to current verified status |
| Team member leaves | Stale affiliation | Update role and photo permissions promptly |
| Customer withdraws permission | Unapproved endorsement remains | Remove affected assets and related claims |

A trust center failing to load should not erase the entire summary. Keep supported public facts available and
provide a contact path for restricted material. Do not copy a stale report into a fallback without its date and scope.

A company acquisition or product rename affects more than the About headline. Check legal entity names, audit
scope, customer permissions, support ownership and historical statements. Preserve meaningful history without
suggesting continuity where responsibilities actually changed.

## Accessibility and localization specifics

**Requirements:** role selectors, accordions and report requests need keyboard access (WCAG 2.1.1) and exposed
names, roles and states (4.1.2). Headings and labels describe their topic or purpose (2.4.6); visual role groupings
need meaningful structure under 1.3.1. Distinguish status through text rather than color alone (1.4.1).

If a report is a PDF or an external portal, the journey does not stop at the link. Include that destination in
the accessibility scope. An HTML summary is useful but may not replace access to the detailed information a
reviewer needs. Disclose format and any access process so people can choose an appropriate path.

**Recommendations:** avoid auto-rotating audience tabs because they change the answer while someone is reading.
A role selector should not move keyboard focus into new content unexpectedly. Keep role labels understandable
without icons; occupation symbols can be culturally ambiguous and stereotyped.

Audience vocabulary needs local research, not only translation. The same job title can mean different authority
or responsibility across organizations and markets. If a title is ambiguous, describe the task or team instead.

Security terminology should preserve precise scope in translation. Have qualified reviewers check claims where
a literal translation might imply certification, legal compliance or a contractual promise not present in the
source. Page language, service region and data location are separate choices and should not be conflated.

Names, pronouns, titles and team order should follow individual preferences and local conventions. Do not require
Western name order or a binary title. Timelines and office hours need locally understandable dates and time zones;
a studio's local clock is optional character, not evidence of response availability.

## Copy by purpose and moment

Audience copy should help visitors recognize a task. In an English illustration, "Coordinate client approvals"
is more informative than "Built for agencies" when the actual difference is approval workflow.
The role label can orient, but the sentence should explain the benefit in that role's context.

Trust copy should state the fact, scope and route to verification. "Access is logged for these administrative
actions" is more useful than "Complete peace of mind" if that is the supported capability. Narrow copy can be
more credible than a broad claim because it lets a reviewer compare the actual control with their requirement.

Restricted-content copy should explain the reason for the boundary and what happens next. "Request the current
report" names an action; nearby text can explain eligibility and response expectations. Avoid "Download" if the
visitor will only enter a sales queue.

About copy should connect a stated value to an observable choice. If the team claims craftsmanship, show how it
reviews work or handles difficult trade-offs. If evidence is not public, use a modest statement rather than an
invented anecdote. Humor may suit the origin story while an outage or request error needs precise language.

## Test the decision, not the impression

Recruit or consult the roles who actually evaluate these pages. Ask an operator to find workflow fit, a reviewer
to identify audit scope, and a candidate to explain the team's working approach. A general audience saying the
page "looks trustworthy" cannot validate the information a technical reviewer needs.

Test a mismatched case too: a visitor whose industry is not listed, whose region is unsupported, or whose policy
requires a control the product lacks. The page should support an informed rejection without forcing a sales call.

Useful measures include correct fit assessment, completion of relevant diligence and appropriately qualified
inquiries. Watch misrouted leads, repeated requests for already available information, unsupported compliance
assumptions and privacy complaints as guardrails.

A shorter sales cycle may reflect a different customer mix rather than better trust content. Pair behavior with
comprehension and support evidence, and report the limits of before-and-after comparisons.

## Worked comparison (fictional design scenario)

**Weak security section:** six certification badges in a row, "Bank-level security" as the headline, no link to
details, one badge for a certification still in progress shown as obtained.

**Strong security section:** the same fictional service presents only facts confirmed by its security owner, with
scope for encryption, storage location, access controls and input use. Completed assurance evidence is named as
the relevant document names it; pending work is separately labeled without promising an unsupported completion date.
A link leads to the current trust material and another to operational status.

The improvement comes from making evidence inspectable and limits visible, not from replacing one grand claim
with another. A residency headline belongs only if the complete statement is supportable, including relevant
processing and transfer exceptions. Otherwise lead with the strongest narrower fact that answers the buyer's doubt.

Test by asking a reviewer which controls are demonstrated, which are merely described and what remains unknown.
If they infer a guarantee that is not present, revise the visual hierarchy and wording, not just the footnote.

## Common mistakes

- **Segmenting by label instead of by problem.** Page quotas and search plans can create empty distinctions.
  Require a different workflow, objection or proof before funding a separate argument.
- **Audience pages that change only the headline.**
- **Badges without meaning, or claimed before they are obtained.** Visual parity can hide different evidence status.
  Make scope, document type and current status visible enough to prevent that inference.
- **Marketing language in security content.**
- **About pages that are only a wall of text, or only stock photos.**

## Checklist

- [ ] Segmentation reflects different problems, proof or objections.
- [ ] Each audience page has its own vocabulary, proof and action.
- [ ] Security content answers concrete questions, plainly, with honest status.
- [ ] Trust center linked; regulations per market accurate.
- [ ] About page has relevant organizational evidence, consent-respecting team information and useful next steps.
- [ ] Claims have owners, dates, scope and update triggers; restricted evidence has a usable request path.
- [ ] Visitors outside listed audiences or supported regions can understand their situation.
- [ ] Audience and technical terminology reviewed in each market, including linked documents.
