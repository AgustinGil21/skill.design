# Applied psychology and ethical persuasion

How a designer uses what is known about attention, memory, decisions, motivation and emotion to help people do what
they want to do — and how they recognize the line where helping becomes manipulating. The ethical line, stated once:
**design so the person can make the decision they would make with full information and time, not the one that suits
us at their expense.**

## What it is and why it matters

Every interface influences behavior. The order of options, the default, the wording of a button, the timing of a
reminder: none of them is neutral. Pretending there is no influence does not remove it; it just makes it unexamined.

Understanding psychology improves design by:

- **Reducing effort** where people are limited (attention, memory, time).
- **Removing friction** from decisions people already want to make.
- **Building trust** through predictability and honesty.
- **Avoiding harm**: recognizing dark patterns, including ones that appear by accident.

## How people process interfaces

- **Attention is limited.** People scan rather than read: headings, the start of paragraphs, bold text, numbers and
  buttons. Design for scanning: the important part at the start of sentences, clear hierarchy, meaningful headings.
- **Working memory is small.** People hold a few things at a time. Group, split into steps, and show instead of asking
  people to remember (recognition over recall).
- **Cognitive load is cumulative.** Every decision, unfamiliar word or competing element costs something. Remove
  before adding.
- **Fluency shapes belief.** Information that is easy to process (good typography, contrast, order) is perceived as
  more trustworthy. This is a reason to design well, and a warning: polish can make weak claims look strong.
- **First impressions form quickly.** Perceived aesthetics influence whether people keep looking. Attractive design is
  forgiven more readily — which helps, and can hide real problems in testing.
- **Context changes capacity.** Stress, fatigue, pain, a second language, a small screen or interruptions all reduce
  the attention and memory available. Design for the realistic context, not for a calm person at a desk.

## Effect observed vs. mechanism assumed

Psychology terms are easy to name and hard to prove. A drop in sign-ups after adding a field is an **observed
effect**; "it's choice overload" is an **assumed mechanism**. The mechanism might be different (the field asks for
data people do not have, it looks invasive, it breaks on mobile).

- Name the effect you observed and the evidence for it.
- Treat the mechanism as a hypothesis, and test it when the decision depends on it.
- Be wary of popular findings applied far from their original context. Many classic results come from specific
  laboratory conditions and do not transfer reliably to every interface.

## How people decide: effects worth understanding

| Effect | What it is | Honest use | Abuse to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Choice overload / Hick** | More options, more time and doubt | Fewer, comparable options; a recommended one with reasons | Hiding the cheaper option |
| **Anchoring** | The first number sets the reference | Show the usual plan or price in context | Invented "was" prices |
| **Decoy effect** | A worse option makes another look better | Genuinely differentiated plans | A plan created only to push another |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses weigh more than equivalent gains | Tell people what they will lose when cancelling, with facts | Guilt-tripping ("No thanks, I like wasting money") |
| **Social proof** | People follow what others do | Real testimonials and real numbers | Invented counters, fake reviews |
| **Scarcity and urgency** | Limited things feel more valuable | Real stock, real deadlines | Countdown timers that reset |
| **Reciprocity** | People return what they receive | Give value before asking (a free tool, a useful trial) | "Gifts" with hidden strings |
| **Commitment and consistency** | People continue what they started | Short steps with visible progress | Asking for little and then charging to leave |
| **Endowment effect** | People value what is theirs | Let people personalize early | Holding data hostage so they cannot leave |
| **Default effect** | Most people keep the default | Defaults that protect the person | Marketing or data sharing on by default |
| **Serial position** | First and last items are remembered | Important items at the start and end | — |
| **Zeigarnik effect** | Unfinished tasks stay in mind | Setup checklists with progress | Constant "you're not done" nagging |
| **Peak-end rule** | Memory favors the peak and the end | Care for moments of achievement and the end of flows | — |
| **Framing** | How something is said changes the choice | "98 % of deliveries on time", if true and measured | Hiding the total cost in friendly framing |
| **Goal gradient** | People speed up near the goal | Show how much is left | Goals that move away as people approach |

## Persuasion, comprehension and autonomy

Three tests separate persuasion from manipulation:

1. **Comprehension.** After the screen, does the person understand what they agreed to, what it costs and how to
   change it? Persuasion that works by preventing understanding is manipulation.
2. **Autonomy.** Can the person choose otherwise with equal ease? If saying no takes three times the effort of saying
   yes, the design is deciding for them.
3. **Reflection.** Would the person endorse the decision later, with full information? If many people regret it
   (refunds, cancellations, complaints), the design pushed them.

A statement can be true and still manipulative. "Only 2 rooms left at this price" may be true for one rate on one
night; shown next to every hotel as a red flashing label, it creates a false sense of scarcity.

## Symmetry between accepting, declining and leaving

A practical rule: **the paths that serve the person must be as easy as the paths that serve the business.**

| Pair | Symmetric | Asymmetric (a dark pattern) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accept / decline cookies | Equal buttons, same level | "Accept all" prominent, "Manage" buried in a second layer |
| Subscribe / cancel | Cancel online, in a similar number of steps | Sign up online, cancel by phone during business hours |
| Opt in / opt out of marketing | Unchecked by default, clear choice | Pre-checked box in small print |
| Upgrade / downgrade | Both explained, both available | Downgrade hidden in support articles |
| Share data / don't share | Equal clarity on both | "Continue" shares; "Don't share" is a text link |

## Dark patterns (do not use)

- **Confirmshaming**: the decline option humiliates ("No, I don't care about my health").
- **Roach motel**: easy to get in, hard to get out.
- **Hidden costs**: fees that appear at the last step.
- **Pre-selection**: marketing, insurance or donations pre-checked.
- **False urgency and scarcity**: timers that reset, "people are looking at this" without data.
- **Disguised ads**: ads that look like content, buttons that look like something else.
- **Nagging**: asking the same thing repeatedly until people give in.
- **Trick questions and confusing interfaces**: double negatives, inverted button weights.
- **Forced continuity**: free trials that convert to paid without clear notice.
- **Privacy tricks**: sharing more than people understood.
- **Interface interference**: making the option the business prefers visually dominant and the other nearly
  invisible.

Beyond being unethical, many of these are illegal in many jurisdictions under consumer protection, privacy and
accessibility law. Regulators increasingly name specific patterns. Do not rely on "everyone does it".

## Vulnerable people and contexts

Persuasion that is acceptable for a confident adult choosing a music subscription can be harmful elsewhere. Apply
extra care when:

- **The audience includes children or teenagers.**
- **The domain involves money, debt, gambling, health, mental health, addiction or dating.**
- **People are in distress**: after a loss, during a crisis, when ill.
- **People have cognitive disabilities** or are reading in a second language.
- **There is a power asymmetry**: an employer's tool, a landlord's portal, a government service people cannot avoid.

In these contexts: no variable rewards, no streak pressure, no urgency, defaults that protect, clear exits, and
reminders people control.

## Motivation and habit, without manipulation

- **Behavior needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment** (a model popularized by BJ Fogg). When
  something does not happen, first make it easier, then remind at the right moment; motivation is the hardest of the
  three to change.
- **Autonomy, competence and relatedness** (self-determination theory): people stay where they choose freely, feel
  capable and feel connected. Designing for these lasts longer than rewards.
- **Rewards**: real progress (what was achieved) beats empty points. Variable rewards create compulsion; do not use
  them in risky contexts (money, health, minors).
- **Habituation and fatigue**: repeated prompts lose effect and become noise. Budget interruptions; let people turn
  them off; stop asking when people decline.

## Emotion and trust

- **Trust is built through predictability**: do what you said, warn before, show the real state (see
  [real state and verification](08-real-state-and-verification.md)).
- **Reduce anxiety in tense moments** (paying, deleting, sending): say what will happen, allow undo where real, confirm
  what was done.
- **Tone follows emotion**: celebrate when the person achieved something; be sober when something failed.
- **Signals of humanity**: real people, names, team photos, signed replies — when real.

## Cultural differences

Persuasive patterns do not work identically everywhere, and some are perceived differently: the weight of social
proof, the acceptability of urgency, attitudes to discounts and haggling, expectations of formality. Do not assume
that a pattern validated in one market transfers to another. Test locally, and avoid patterns that are borderline in
any market.

## How to use this in practice

1. **Identify the decision** the person has to make on the screen.
2. **List what holds them back**: doubt, fear, effort, distrust, missing information.
3. **Resolve each obstacle with information, proof or ease**, not with pressure.
4. **Check symmetry**: are the paths that serve the person as easy as the ones that serve the business?
5. **Check comprehension**: could the person explain what they agreed to?
6. **Measure regret, not just conversion**: refunds, cancellations, complaints, support contacts, opt-outs in the
   following weeks.
7. **Ask the public test**: would we be comfortable explaining this design to a journalist, a regulator, or the
   person's family?

## Worked comparison

**Same situation:** a meal-kit subscription's cancellation flow. The business wants to retain customers; the person
wants to cancel.

**Manipulative version.**

- "Cancel" is hidden under Account › Plan › Manage › More options.
- Step 1: "Are you sure? You'll lose all your saved recipes forever!" (they are not deleted).
- Step 2: a survey with required questions.
- Step 3: an offer with "Keep my discount" as a large button and "I still want to leave" as small grey text.
- Step 4: "Call us to complete your cancellation."

**Honest version.**

- "Cancel subscription" is visible in Plan settings.
- One screen: what happens ("Your last box arrives on 14 March. You won't be charged after that. Your saved recipes
  stay in your account."), an optional pause alternative ("Pause for up to 8 weeks instead"), an optional one-question
  reason, and two buttons of equal weight: "Pause instead" and "Cancel subscription".
- A confirmation with the end date, by email too.

The honest version still offers a retention option — pausing is often what people actually want — but it informs
instead of tricking, and it can be measured by retention *and* by the absence of complaints and chargebacks.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming a bias as if it explained the behavior**, without evidence.
- **Copying growth tactics** from other products without checking whether they are honest.
- **Optimizing a single metric** (conversion) and ignoring regret and trust.
- **Asymmetric paths** justified as "retention".
- **Ignoring vulnerable contexts.**
- **Using the "would it bother me?" test alone**: the designer is rarely the most vulnerable user.

## Checklist

- [ ] The decision on the screen and what holds people back are identified.
- [ ] Obstacles are resolved with information, proof or ease, not pressure.
- [ ] Accepting, declining and leaving are symmetric.
- [ ] No dark patterns; claims of scarcity, urgency and social proof are real.
- [ ] Extra care was applied for vulnerable audiences and sensitive domains.
- [ ] Defaults protect the person.
- [ ] Prompts and reminders are budgeted and controllable.
- [ ] Success is measured with regret and trust signals, not only conversion.
