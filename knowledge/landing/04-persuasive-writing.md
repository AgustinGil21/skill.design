# Persuasive writing (without fluff)

Develop the message before polishing the layout, then refine writing and composition together. This file covers
how to get language from evidence, build a message hierarchy, calibrate claims, write by objection, and adapt
persuasion across languages and cultures. Principles are stated by function; English examples illustrate them.

For interface copy, see [interface writing](../04-interface-writing.md); for writing in other languages, see
[content and localization](../24-content-and-localization.md).

## What it is and why it matters

Persuasive copy helps a visitor decide by making the value clear, credible and relevant to them. It is not about
clever words: the most persuasive copy usually repeats the customer's own description of their problem and shows
specific proof that it gets solved.

## From evidence to messages

Customer language is a useful starting point; synthesis still requires judgment about scope and relevance.

1. **Collect language**: sales calls, support tickets, reviews (yours and competitors'), interviews, search queries.
2. **Note the exact phrases** people use for their problem, their desired outcome and their objections.
3. **Group them**: problems, outcomes, objections, decision criteria.
4. **Build a message hierarchy**:

| Level | Content | Example (clinic scheduling) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core promise | The main outcome | "Fewer no-shows and fewer phone calls" |
| Supporting points | How it delivers | Online booking · text reminders · patient records |
| Proof per point | Evidence | Named clinic results · screenshots · integrations |
| Objection answers | Barriers removed | Free import · price per practitioner · EU hosting |

5. **Write from the hierarchy**: the hero carries the core promise; sections carry supporting points with proof.

## Formulas as starting points

- **Outcome + for whom**: "Answer all your customers in one place."
- **The problem named**: "Your website is a static document. It shouldn't be." (Ploy)
- **The change**: "From idea to assets in four steps." (Fourmula AI)
- **The contradiction**: "All the answers, none of the headaches." (Maze)
- **The category name** only when it already exists in the reader's mind.

Write many headlines, keep two, test them with someone outside the team.

## Calibrating claims

The numerical examples below are fictional illustrations of claim structure, not substantiated public claims.

Every claim is a promise. Calibrate it to what is true:

| Weak (absolute or unverifiable) | Calibrated (verifiable) |
| --- | --- |
| "Never lose a message again" | "Every message from email, chat and WhatsApp in one inbox" |
| "10× faster" | "Reports that took a day now take about an hour, according to Lisboa Coffee's finance team" |
| "The best scheduling tool" | "Rated 4.8 by 1,200 clinics on [review site]" (if true) |
| "Revolutionize your finances" | "Close the month in 2 days" (if measured) |

Keep a **claims register**: each public claim with its source, date and owner, reviewed when products or data change.

### Benefit vs. absolute promise

"Benefit first, feature second" is good advice, but a benefit can become an unjustifiable promise. "Never miss a
message" sounds like a benefit and is a guarantee the product cannot make. Prefer benefits the product can stand
behind.

## Rules

- **Concrete beats grandiose**: "Close the month in 2 days" > "Revolutionize your finances".
- **Numbers with units and context**: "4.2 million impressions in one month", not "millions of impressions".
- **Benefit first, feature second** — calibrated as above.
- **Address one person**, in the form of address appropriate to the language and audience, not "users".
- **Buttons name the outcome in the language’s natural grammar**: "Create my account", "See pricing", "Try it with my
  data".
- **Answer the objection where it appears**: next to the button, "No card required"; next to the price, "Cancel
  anytime".
- **Short sentences, paragraphs of one to three lines**, one idea per paragraph.
- **Proof before adjectives**: if you need to say "powerful", a demonstration is missing.

## Writing by objection

The clinic and quantities below are fictional examples; replace them with approved evidence before publication.

For each major objection (from [strategy](01-strategy-and-structure.md)), write: the objection in the visitor's words,
the answer, and the proof. Place it where the objection arises.

> Objection: "We'd have to move 3,000 patients."
> Answer: "We import your patients from [system] and spreadsheets for free."
> Proof: "Fisio Norte moved 4,200 patients in a morning." (with permission)

## Tone by risk

- **Low stakes** (a design tool, a game): more playful, more personality.
- **High stakes** (money, health, security, legal): calm, precise, respectful; humor sparingly or never.
- **Audience sophistication**: experts want specifics and dislike hype; newcomers need plain explanations.

## Words and devices to avoid

The categories below exist in every language; English examples illustrate them. The slop detector
(`scripts/slop.mjs`) has per-language lists.

- **Hollow verbs and adjectives**: supercharge, revolutionize, next-generation, seamless, all-in-one, the future of,
  unlock, transform, empower, integrated solution, world-class.
- **Adjective triads** ("Simple. Fast. Secure.").
- **Chains of rhetorical questions** ("Tired of…? Want…?").
- **Capitals and exclamation marks** for energy.
- **False urgency** ("Today only!") and invented scarcity.
- **Unsourced comparisons** ("10× faster").

## Persuasion across languages and cultures

- **Write natively per market**: persuasion depends on idiom, rhythm and register. A headline translated word for word
  often sounds like a translation.
- **Directness varies**: some cultures prefer direct claims; others find them pushy and prefer understatement or
  social proof.
- **Proof varies**: local customers, local media, local certifications persuade more than foreign logos.
- **Calls to action** follow the language's conventions (imperative vs. infinitive, formality).
- **Humor and wordplay** rarely translate; replace them with market-specific equivalents or plain statements.
- **Legal constraints** on claims (comparative advertising, health claims, financial promotions) differ by country.

## Manifesto or letter

For brands with a point of view, a signed first-person letter can persuade more than a feature grid ("To everyone who
buys sneakers…" signed by the founder;
[Zellerfeld](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/09a9a70b-70f6-49d9-8966-d5524e46f120)).
It works when it is specific and honest; not when it reads like a press release.

## Microcopy that matters

- **The email field label** says what it is for ("Your work email").
- **What happens after submitting** ("We'll send a link; it arrives in about a minute").
- **Alternative text** for each image describes what it proves, not "product screenshot".
- **Page titles and meta descriptions** written, not generated automatically.

## Worked comparison: a rewrite with reasoning

This restaurant scenario and its quantities are fictional; the time comparison requires actual measurement.

**Before**: "Supercharge your team's productivity with our all-in-one, AI-powered platform. Simple. Fast. Secure. Get
started today!"

**After**: "Plan the week's shifts in 10 minutes, not two hours. Restaurant managers drag staff into shifts, the app
checks availability and labor rules, and everyone gets their schedule by text. Try it free for 14 days — no card."

What changed:

- Hollow verbs and the triad replaced by a concrete outcome with a time comparison (that must be measured).
- The audience named (restaurant managers).
- How it works in one sentence.
- The objection (cost, commitment) answered next to the action.
- The exclamation removed.

## Testing copy

- **Comprehension**: show the hero and ask what the product does and for whom.
- **Credibility**: ask which claims they believe and which they doubt.
- **Expectations**: after reading, ask what they expect to happen after clicking — and compare with what happens.
- **Comparison tests** with the target audience: ask what each headline means before asking which they prefer.

## Questions before choosing a promise

- What is the visitor trying to accomplish, and what can this product actually change in that task?
- Which outcome depends on their effort, third parties or market conditions rather than the product alone?
- What did they read before arriving, and which qualification must survive into the page?
- What evidence supports the claim for this audience, plan, region and product version?
- Does the person choosing also perform the work? Which vocabulary is shared between those roles?
- Which misunderstanding would produce a costly sign-up, failed implementation or unwanted commitment?
- Is the objection emotional, procedural or factual? What evidence could realistically answer it?
- Which parts of the message are brand expression, and which must remain exact across markets?

Do not promote the most vivid interview quote into a universal promise. Record who said it, in what situation and
whether other evidence supports the underlying need. Customer wording can reveal a problem while still containing
incorrect assumptions about the product. Preserve the need, not the error.

## Alternatives and their costs

| Situation | Message approach | Why | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Visitors know the task | Specific outcome | Establishes relevance quickly | Needs bounded evidence |
| Category is unfamiliar | Mechanism plus outcome | Explains how value happens | Takes more space |
| Switching dominates | Migration and compatibility | Answers practical risk | Less emotionally distinctive |
| Familiar brand | Expressive line plus explanation | Uses existing recognition | Weak for unknown audiences |
| Evidence is early | Capability and evaluation invitation | Avoids unsupported results | Less dramatic promise |
| Several stakeholders | Shared outcome with role detail | Supports internal discussion | Needs hierarchy |

A problem-agitation sequence can help people recognize an overlooked cost, but is inappropriate when it magnifies
anxiety without adding useful understanding. A visitor already dealing with illness, debt or service failure may
need calm next steps. Even in low-stakes products, guilt and invented urgency undermine informed choice.

A manifesto is suitable when the founder's position explains actual product choices. It is less useful when the
visitor needs compatibility, cost or delivery information now. Keep practical facts reachable rather than making
agreement with the brand philosophy a prerequisite for evaluation.

## An evidence workflow for claims

1. Write the proposed claim in full, including the implication a reasonable visitor might infer.
2. Identify its type: capability, observed result, comparison, forecast, endorsement or guarantee.
3. Attach the source, collection context, date, permission and the person responsible for verification.
4. Record scope: which plan, market, cohort, conditions and exclusions the evidence covers.
5. Compare the wording with that scope; narrow it when the source cannot support the implied breadth.
6. Place material conditions beside the claim, with deeper methodology available where appropriate.
7. Define an expiry or review trigger tied to the thing that could make the claim wrong.
8. Review the complete page for contradictory promises, including buttons, images and confirmation messages.

A disclaimer cannot repair a headline that communicates the opposite. If a result only applies under a specific
condition, that condition belongs in the meaningful reading path. Legal approval and comprehension testing answer
different questions; neither substitutes for the other.

When evidence is absent, replace a result claim with an inspectable capability or an explicit invitation to test.
An internal draft can carry a marked evidence placeholder. A public page should not silently turn that placeholder
into a rounded estimate, an invented testimonial or an unsourced superlative.

## Copy through states and edge cases

| Moment | Function | Fictional English illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before action | Predict the destination | "View the sample schedule" |
| Required condition | Explain the constraint | "A manager account is needed to publish" |
| Loading | Describe current work | "Preparing the sample schedule" |
| Partial result | Bound what is available | "The sample loaded; availability checks are unavailable" |
| Recoverable failure | Explain a useful next step | "The sample could not load. Try again or read the walkthrough" |
| Uncertain submission | Avoid false confirmation | "Request unconfirmed. Check status before retrying" |
| Confirmed success | State the actual consequence | "Your request was received. The next step is choosing a time" |
| Unavailable market | Explain fit honestly | "This service is not available in your selected region" |

These examples illustrate functions, not reusable promises. Match the actual system behavior: do not say a request
was received unless that is confirmed. When no delivery estimate exists, avoid inventing reassuring timing.

Long organization names, mixed scripts and plural counts expose weak sentence construction. Localize complete
messages with variables that can move. Avoid surrounding a person's name with gendered assumptions. A neutral
construction may be clearer than collecting personal attributes merely to make promotional text sound personal.

## Accessibility requirements and editorial recommendations

WCAG 2.4.4 Link Purpose (In Context) (A) requires a link's purpose to be determinable from its text or permitted
context. Repeated generic links can work with adequate context, but specific destination labels usually make
scanning easier. The latter is a recommendation, not a requirement to ban the words "Learn more".

WCAG 2.4.6 Headings and Labels (AA) requires headings and labels to describe their topic or purpose. A poetic
heading can coexist with a descriptive explanation, but test whether the heading structure alone helps someone
navigate. WCAG 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (A) applies where input requires labels or instructions.

WCAG 3.1.1 Language of Page (A) and 3.1.2 Language of Parts (AA) cover programmatically determining language,
subject to the latter's exceptions. A language selector does not by itself satisfy either criterion. Localized
pages and embedded quotations need appropriate implementation as well as accurate translation.

Plain language, shorter paragraphs and familiar terminology are strong recommendations for broad audiences.
WCAG 3.1.5 Reading Level is AAA, not an AA requirement that all marketing copy meet an arbitrary grade level.
Technical vocabulary can be necessary for expert decisions; explain it where newcomers also need the page.

Do not encode important copy only in an image. WCAG 1.4.5 Images of Text (AA) has exceptions, including essential
presentation, but editable text usually adapts better to zoom, translation and assistive technology.

## Localization review as message review

Brief a local writer on the intended outcome, evidence, exclusions and audience before asking for variants. Give
them permission to change rhythm, idiom and word order while retaining factual scope. A literal translation can
preserve every word and still imply a stronger promise through the target language's conventions.

Check the form of address in persuasive and recovery moments together. A familiar greeting followed by an
unexpectedly formal failure message can feel like a different service. Do not infer cultural preferences from
national stereotypes; review with people who know the specific market and audience.

Numbers need units, time windows and appropriate separators. Dates need a locale-appropriate representation and
an unambiguous meaning. Currency and tax treatment depend on the offer's market rather than the interface language.
Local testimonials need their own permission and scope; translating a result does not make it representative.

## Worked comparison when the evidence is missing

Return to the fictional restaurant scheduling product above. Suppose the team cannot substantiate the proposed
speed comparison and labor-rule coverage varies by region. Keeping the dramatic claim with a footnote would leave
the strongest impression unsupported and might imply compliance the product cannot guarantee.

A stronger alternative is: "Build the week's shifts around staff availability." Supporting copy explains that
managers assign people to shifts, review flagged conflicts and publish the schedule. The action names a sample
schedule. Region-specific rule support is stated where someone evaluates that feature, with its limits visible.

This version gives up the striking time-saving comparison. It gains a claim the visitor can inspect and avoids
confusing assistance with a guarantee. If reliable evidence later supports a narrower time claim, add it with the
conditions rather than replacing the whole message hierarchy.

## Verification and measurement

Test understanding before preference. Ask participants to explain the offer in their own words, identify what is
included, describe the commitment and predict the next screen. Do not ask whether the copy is "clear"; people may
agree while holding an inaccurate interpretation. Record the specific inference and the phrase that prompted it.

Use contrasting realistic tasks: a qualified visitor, someone missing a requirement and someone comparing costs.
Successful writing should help each make an informed choice. Fewer unsuitable leads can be an improvement even
when top-level conversion falls. Define qualified progression and mismatch reasons before launch.

Guardrails include cancellation caused by misunderstood terms, support questions about promised capabilities,
unwanted-contact complaints and abandonment after discovering a condition. Segment by source and locale; a
headline test does not establish that the same promise works for another language or acquisition channel.

Review claims when the product, evidence or market changes. Assign ownership to reusable snippets so the hero,
pricing table, ad and success message do not drift into different offers. A copy inventory matters most at those
boundaries, where a sentence can remain grammatically correct while becoming operationally false.

## Common mistakes

Absolute promises often appear because internal differentiation is weak or an unsupported headline sounds more
confident. Return to the inspectable mechanism and narrow the scope. Copy written last usually inherits arbitrary
space constraints; test the actual message in the layout before protecting an idealized text length.

- **Writing from the product's features** instead of the customer's language.
- **Absolute promises.**
- **Translated instead of written per market.**
- **Copy written last** to fill boxes.

## Checklist

- [ ] Language collected from customers; message hierarchy written.
- [ ] Claims calibrated and recorded with sources.
- [ ] Objections answered where they arise, with proof.
- [ ] Tone matches risk and audience sophistication.
- [ ] No hollow words, triads, false urgency or unsourced comparisons.
- [ ] Written natively for each market.
- [ ] Tested for comprehension, credibility and expectations.

- [ ] State messages match confirmed system behavior, including uncertain outcomes.
- [ ] Language, labels and link purposes meet the applicable accessibility criteria.
- [ ] Qualification and downstream mismatch are measured, not just action clicks.
