# The hero (first screen)

The first screen should quickly make clear what this is, for whom, and what to do. Everything else is how it is
said. The hero is the most scrutinized part of a landing page, and the most often over-designed: the more visual
effort goes into it, the easier it is to forget that its job is comprehension.

## What it is and why it matters

The hero is the first impression and the first argument. It decides whether visitors continue, act or leave. It
must:

- **Continue the promise** that brought the visitor (the ad, the search, the link).
- **Say what the product is and does**, in the visitor's words.
- **Show it**, when the product can be shown.
- **Offer the next step** that fits the visitor's intent.
- **Give a first reason to believe.**

## The hero by visitor intent

The same product needs different heroes for different visitors (see [strategy](01-strategy-and-structure.md)):

| Visitor | Needs first | Hero emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware of the category | What this is | Explain the category and outcome plainly |
| Problem-aware (searched the problem) | That this solves their problem | Name the problem and the result |
| Comparing | Why this one | The difference, with proof |
| Ready to act | How to start | Price, action, low risk |
| Returning or existing customer | What's new | The new value, specifically |

### When to explain the category

If visitors do not know what kind of product this is, a clever headline ("Make it real") without a plain subheading
leaves them guessing. Either the headline or the subheading must state the category and outcome in plain words
("Vizcom helps industrial designers turn sketches into realistic renders").

## The pieces

- **Headline**: the outcome or the idea, in the largest size on the page. It can be concrete ("Precise product
  images, simplified"; [Vizcom](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/5a194a1a-1add-434f-b901-b832b3c4ba78)) or a phrase
  with character backed by the subheading ("Make it real",
  [Vizcom](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/99cd0122-9e27-415e-ba45-b46f423c25b4)). Word count is a guide, not a
  goal: what matters is that it is understood quickly.
- **Subheading**: one or two lines saying what it does and for whom, in plain language.
- **Primary and secondary action**: "Start" and "Book a demo"; usually with one visually dominant action. Low-risk
  text nearby
  ("Free, no card"; "Free, then 3 months at 1 USD";
  [Shopify](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/c26f1905-9dc4-476d-9627-c036a2399324)).
- **The visual**: the real product (screenshot, short video, 3D object) or a visual idea of its own. A gradient needs
  a brand or communication purpose.
- **Nearby proof**: logos, a figure, ratings, "18.9k stars on GitHub"
  ([Dub](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/11da46b5-35aa-4750-96cb-b36276f8d722)) — real and verifiable.

### Headline, proof and visual work together

Decide which of the three carries the message:

| Lead | When | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Headline-led** | The idea is strong and clear in words | Generic if the words are vague |
| **Visual-led** | Value is visible in the product | Confusing if the visual needs explanation |
| **Proof-led** | Credibility is the main barrier | Weak if the proof is not compelling |

### Hierarchy between actions

Two actions can coexist when they serve genuinely different intents (ready to try vs. need more information). Decide
which leads based on the main audience, and make the other clearly secondary. A third action at the same level is
almost always an undecided hierarchy.

## Compositions that work

- **Centered text with a large screenshot below** that peeks above the fold and invites scrolling
  ([Craft](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/81874e27-d807-48c6-8bf3-c2efd62b8c2f)). Safe and clear; it needs
  identity through typography and the visual.
- **Text on one side, visual on the other**, or photos floating around
  ([Maze](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/692025e8-77b8-45c7-9f60-3ee6d44e0688)): good for longer phrases.
- **Typography as image**: a huge headline interacting with the object (the car sketch in front of the letters,
  Vizcom). Memorable when the concept justifies it.
- **Capability tabs in the hero** (Automate · Create · Analyze) that change the demo, with a pause button for any
  automatic rotation ([Sana](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/7f35e77e-e242-4333-809d-49fc3156ba43)).
- **Photo or video of real people using the product**, with a scrim for text contrast (Shopify).
- **An email field in the hero** when the action is single and short ("Your work email?" + "Start free";
  [Ramp](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/c224c647-a876-44fc-ad18-ac01acee3004)) — with error and success states
  designed (see [conversion forms](13-conversion-forms.md)).
- **Live data** that proves scale without saying it ("Agents working today: 774,485", Ramp) — only if real.

## What ruins it

- A category headline ("Integrated platform for…") or an empty one ("The future of work").
- A three-line subheading with three adjectives.
- An automatic message carousel that hides essential information before people can read it.
- Background video that covers the text, has no pause and weighs 20 MB.
- A fake screenshot, or one so small it cannot be read.
- An entrance animation that makes people wait to read the headline.
- A subscription pop-up after three seconds.

## Mobile and translation

- **Mobile first screen**: prioritize headline, explanation and action rather than forcing a fixed fold;
  the visual can follow. Test with large system text.
- **Translation**: headlines that rely on wordplay rarely survive translation. Write each market's headline natively,
  and test the layout with the longest language.
- **Line breaks**: avoid hard-coded breaks that only work in one language; use balanced wrapping.

## States for heroes with interaction

Heroes with an email field, a live demo or a configurator need their states designed: loading, invalid input, success
("Check your email"), error, already registered. A hero that breaks on the first interaction loses the visitor at the
most valuable moment.

## Three solutions for the same product

**Fictional example product:** an expense app for small teams; all proposed copy and quantities are illustrative.

1. **Headline-led**: "Expenses done before the month ends." / "Snap receipts, approve in one tap, export to your
   accountant." / Start free · See how it works / screenshot of the approval screen.
2. **Visual-led**: a large phone photographing a receipt that becomes a structured expense in real time / "From receipt
   to report in seconds" / Start free.
3. **Proof-led**: "Used by 3,200 small teams in Portugal and Spain" (if true) with logos / "Expenses, approvals and
   exports for teams of 2 to 50" / Start free.

Choosing among them depends on the visitor: a problem-aware search ("expense report tool") favors 1; social ads to
people unfamiliar with the category favor 2; a risk-averse audience (finance managers) favors 3.

## Comprehension protocol

Use a brief exposure as a diagnostic, then allow normal reading and ask what it is, for whom, and what happens next.
A five-second exercise is a convention, not a validated pass threshold. Separate initial recall from considered
understanding; complex purchases may reasonably need longer. Repeat with relevant audiences and languages.

## Questions before choosing the first argument

A hero cannot summarize every audience equally well. Identify the visitor whose misunderstanding would be most
costly, then decide what other visitors need to recognize their route. Existing customers may need sign-in more
than a new promise; a campaign arrival may need confirmation that an advertised capability is actually included.

- What exact promise appears immediately before this page, including its qualification and offer conditions?
- Does the visitor know the category, or only the problem? Which words do they already use for it?
- Is the buyer also the operator? If not, which role must feel recognized before forwarding the page?
- What must be understood before action: availability, commitment, eligibility, price, or data access?
- Which claim can be demonstrated now, and which is only an aspiration or a roadmap item?
- Can the visual explain the outcome at phone size, or does it depend on reading a desktop interface?
- What does the primary action actually open, and can the visitor complete that destination in their market?
- Which uncertainty would change the composition rather than merely change a sentence?

Record the answer and its source. Search terms indicate intent but do not establish purchasing authority; sales
requests reveal objections but overrepresent people who already persevered. Label unresolved assumptions instead
of treating internal enthusiasm as evidence of visitor understanding.

## Alternatives and what each gives up

| Situation | Option | Why | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distinct workflow | Outcome plus product crop | Makes the difference inspectable | Needs current imagery |
| Unknown category | Plain explanation plus sequence | Establishes a mental model | Uses more reading space |
| High commitment | Evidence plus qualified promise | Reduces uncertainty | Proof may require context |
| Several audiences | Shared promise plus route choices | Helps visitors find their task | Adds a decision |
| Campaign with narrow intent | Focused hero and direct action | Preserves continuity | Less useful as a homepage |
| Utility or status page | Compact title and task controls | Gets people to work | Gives up a promotional moment |

An oversized promotional hero is not required for every entry page. If someone arrives to check eligibility or
find a document, a concise title and the relevant tool may serve better. A cinematic introduction can fit a launch
experience whose audience expects discovery; it needs an immediate route to practical information.

Do not lead with a proof figure merely because it is large. An install count answers adoption, not reliability.
When no credible proof exists, show the mechanism, state its limits, and offer a reversible way to evaluate it.
An honest product example is more useful than a row of aspirational customer logos.

## States and recovery at the first interaction

| State | What the visitor needs | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial | Identify the offer | Render the proposition before optional media |
| Media loading | Continue evaluating | Keep a useful poster and descriptive text |
| Media unavailable | Understand the same claim | Offer a static sequence or explanation |
| Form invalid | Correct the specific issue | Associate the explanation with the field |
| Submitting | Know the request is in progress | Preserve input and prevent accidental repeats |
| Result uncertain | Avoid duplicate requests | Say confirmation is pending; offer status checking |
| Success | Know the next concrete step | Explain the destination or expected contact |
| Existing account | Continue without starting over | Provide a relevant sign-in or recovery route |
| Market unavailable | Decide whether to continue | State availability before collecting unnecessary data |
| Session expired | Resume safely | Re-establish the session without losing useful input |

A polished hero must not imply success just because a button changed color. If a demo booking request reaches a
server but confirmation fails, distinguish that uncertain result from a request known not to have been sent.
Avoid account-existence disclosures where the service's privacy model requires a neutral response.

## Accessibility decisions in the hero

WCAG requirements depend on the conformance target. The following are requirements at the stated levels, rather
than optional advice about how a hero should look:

- 1.1.1 Non-text Content (A): provide alternatives for meaningful imagery; decorative imagery needs no narration.
- 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (AA): text contrast applies over every relevant background frame, not just the poster.
- 1.4.10 Reflow (AA): keep content usable at narrow equivalent widths, subject to the criterion's exceptions.
- 2.1.1 Keyboard (A): hero tabs, players and forms must be operable without a pointing device.
- 2.4.7 Focus Visible (AA): interactive controls need a visible keyboard focus indicator.
- 3.3.1 Error Identification (A): identify detected input errors in text, not only through color.
- 4.1.3 Status Messages (AA): applicable status updates must be programmatically available without moving focus.

As a recommendation, keep the heading and action stable while media loads. Layout stability supports comprehension
and prevents accidental clicks; a specific hero height is not a WCAG requirement. Avoid forcing all content above
an imagined fold by shrinking text. Device chrome, zoom and translated copy make the fold variable.

A screenshot alternative should state the relevant capability, not enumerate every decorative pixel. When details
are necessary to assess the claim, provide nearby structured text or a larger view with an accessible way back.

## Copy and localization at the decision point

The heading establishes relevance, the supporting sentence explains the mechanism, and the action predicts the
next step. In English, a hypothetical label such as "Explore the demo" distinguishes evaluation from registration.
The equivalent in another language should preserve that distinction rather than preserve English verb order.

- Translate the proposition and eligibility together; a local headline must not promise an unavailable feature.
- Choose address and formality for the audience and moment, including mixed buyer and operator audiences.
- Test scripts without spaces, longer compounds, diacritics and right-to-left text before approving fixed crops.
- Keep meaningful text as text where possible; screenshots may need localized variants and an update owner.
- Format prices, dates and trial durations for the chosen market; language alone does not identify currency.
- Keep variable phrases translatable as complete messages, with plural and agreement rules handled by the locale.

Do not automatically mirror a physical product or its branding in right-to-left layouts. Mirror the reading flow
where appropriate, and review directional controls individually. A diagram of an actual object may need to retain
its physical orientation while labels move.

## Worked comparison: the same expense product

This is a fictional teaching example, not evidence of conversion improvement. The product captures receipts,
routes expenses for approval and exports records for an accountant; no measured time-saving claim is available.

**Weak version:** "Make money management effortless" sits over a decorative finance animation. Two equal buttons,
"Get started" and "Discover more", lead to registration and a video respectively. The receipt screen is too small
to inspect, and the supporting line implies that the whole accounting process is automated.

**Stronger version:** "Collect receipts and approve team expenses in one place" establishes the task. Supporting
copy explains capture, review and export, while a readable crop shows a receipt awaiting approval. The main action
names the trial destination; a quieter demo link serves visitors who need to inspect the workflow first.

The change narrows the claim to what exists, makes the operator recognizable, and lets the image support a specific
sentence. It gives up a broad emotional promise. That is a sensible trade when misunderstanding creates poor-fit
sign-ups, but a familiar brand campaign might retain a more expressive headline with this explanation underneath.

Test whether people understand that an accountant still uses the export. If they infer automatic filing, improve
the mechanism sentence before optimizing button prominence. A stronger-looking hero can still promise the wrong
service, and aesthetic preference does not resolve that error.

## Verification beyond first impressions

Use representative arrival contexts: show the preceding ad or search snippet before the hero. Ask participants to
explain what they think is included and predict the result of selecting the primary action. Then let them follow
it. Record mismatches between expectation and destination, including those who correctly decide not to proceed.

Compare qualified next-step completion with hero action rate. More clicks accompanied by more immediate exits or
support questions can indicate ambiguity. Segment by source, device and market before attributing changes to the
headline; an audience mix change can produce the same aggregate movement.

Inspect the page with media blocked, enlarged text, keyboard navigation and slow loading. Check both the message
and the destination. A high-performing hero that sends an unavailable market into a dead end has not solved the
visitor's problem. Keep claim ownership and offer expiry in the handoff so the argument stays truthful after launch.

## Common mistakes

Hero overdesign often starts with a stakeholder asking for more impact before the proposition is settled. Review
the claim, visual and destination together so visual polish cannot conceal a mismatch. Equal-weight actions often
reflect competing teams; resolve the audience priority rather than styling each request as equally important.

- **Designing the visual before the message.**
- **Clever headlines without plain explanation.**
- **Equal-weight actions.**
- **Heavy, unpausable media.**
- **Untested on mobile and in other languages.**

## Checklist

- [ ] The hero continues the promise of the main traffic source.
- [ ] What it is, for whom and the outcome are clear in plain words.
- [ ] One lead element (headline, visual or proof) chosen deliberately.
- [ ] One primary action, at most one secondary, with low-risk text.
- [ ] Real product or a real visual idea; real, verifiable proof.
- [ ] Works on mobile, with large text and in the longest language.
- [ ] Interactive states designed.
- [ ] Passed a five-second comprehension test.

- [ ] The claim’s evidence, scope and owner are recorded.
- [ ] Media failure, unavailable markets and uncertain submissions have useful recovery.
- [ ] Qualified progression and expectation mismatches are measured alongside clicks.
